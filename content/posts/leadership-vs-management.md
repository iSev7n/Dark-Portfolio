# Leadership vs Management: Why the Difference Matters More Than Ever

If you have been in a formation long enough, you have seen it happen.

A team starts slipping. A problem keeps repeating. Morale drops. People get quiet. Standards get harder to enforce. The initial response is almost always the same.

More trackers. More meetings. More slides. More “visibility.” More checklists. More control.

It looks like action. It feels like accountability.

But a lot of the time, it is not leadership showing up. It is management expanding to fill a gap.

CPT Charles R. Gallagher described this problem clearly in his Military Review piece, *Muddling Leadership and Management in the United States Army*. His point was not that management is bad or leadership is good. His point was that the Army often treats them as the same thing, and when we do that, we dilute leadership and try to solve dynamic problems with the wrong tools.

That distinction is not theoretical.

It shapes trust, performance, morale, retention, and the long-term stability of any organization.

---

## Gallagher’s warning: the Army is prone to manage “wicked problems”

One of Gallagher’s strongest arguments is that the Army tends to handle complex, human problems through management processes.

He calls these “wicked problems” and points out they are not solved through checklists and compliance alone. Wicked problems are messy, social, and evolving. They require influence, trust, and commitment over time, not just control.

His critique is simple: when we use management techniques for uncertain problems, the results look organized, but the underlying issue stays alive.

That is why many organizations become “over-managed and under-led,” a phrase he uses to describe what happens when leadership gets replaced by process.

---

## Understanding management

Management exists to provide structure.

Its responsibility is execution and control of complexity. Gallagher references a UK military definition that describes management as the “allocation and control of resources” to meet objectives under time constraints. That wording matters because it highlights what management is built on.

Management is primarily positional.

It answers practical questions:

- What needs to be done?
- Who is responsible?
- When is it due?
- What standard defines acceptable performance?
- How is success measured?

Management organizes effort. It reduces ambiguity. It creates repeatable systems so outcomes are predictable instead of chaotic.

Gallagher uses a familiar example: the command and staff meeting staring at spreadsheets to ensure administrative coverage. That is real management. It matters. It keeps the machine running.

But he also makes the point that meetings and trackers, no matter how well executed, do not automatically create change. They keep routine functions organized. They do not build belief.

---

## Understanding leadership

Leadership is centered on people.

Its responsibility is purpose, direction, and motivation.

Gallagher references the Army’s doctrinal definition of leadership as influencing people through “purpose, direction, and motivation.” He emphasizes the word *process* because leadership is interactive behavior between leader and follower.

That matters because it means leadership is not only tied to rank.

He highlights the idea of the “emergent leader,” the Soldier without formal authority who still leads in a firefight because leadership is behavior, not a billet.

Gallagher also references John Kotter’s view that leadership is about establishing direction, aligning people, motivating, and inspiring. Those are leadership actions, and they are consistently different from management.

Where management creates order, leadership creates belief.

Without leadership, teams can continue functioning, but they function mechanically.

Initiative fades. Creativity declines. Work becomes transactional. People comply instead of commit.

---

## Why the confusion keeps happening

Gallagher makes a blunt point that is uncomfortable because it is true.

Army doctrine does not clearly define management in the same way it defines leadership. He notes that ADRP 1-02 and Joint Publication 1-02 did not include a doctrinal definition of management in his analysis.

So what happens in practice?

Leadership and management merge into one concept. Leadership becomes described in managerial terms. The scope of leadership shrinks because management is tied to authority and control.

Gallagher’s takeaway is that this creates confusion for leaders in command positions. You start spending time and energy without a clear sense of whether you are actually leading or just managing harder.

Over time, that confusion pushes organizations into the safest default behavior.

If you cannot lead your way through uncertainty, you manage it.

---

## When management replaces leadership

When leadership is absent, management expands.

It usually shows up as:

- Micromanagement
- Excessive oversight
- Rigid adherence to process
- Fear-based accountability
- Overreliance on metrics and compliance

This can increase short-term output.

It rarely increases long-term commitment.

Gallagher’s “wicked problem” argument fits here. When the issue is cultural, behavioral, or trust-based, a management-heavy approach often produces what looks like progress, but mostly creates fatigue.

People meet requirements.
They stop volunteering effort.
They protect themselves instead of improving the system.

Compliance increases.
Ownership disappears.

And ownership is what drives real performance.

---

## When leadership replaces management

The opposite imbalance is just as damaging.

Leadership without management creates ambiguity.

Vision without structure produces confusion.

Good intentions cannot replace:

- Clear standards
- Defined responsibilities
- Measurable expectations
- Coordinated execution

Gallagher is clear that the Army needs both. His argument is not “stop managing.” It is “stop pretending management is leadership.”

If we only led, we would be inspired but dysfunctional. If we only managed, we would be efficient but directionless.

Both extremes break teams.

---

## Command is where the Army blends them, and sometimes muddies them

Gallagher points out something important about how the Army thinks.

Command is positional authority. Leadership is not necessarily positional. Management is primarily positional. The commander is expected to do all of it, and that is where confusion starts.

When command gets interpreted mostly through management, you end up with an organization that feels controlled but not connected.

You can have standards. You can have compliance. You can have “green” metrics.

And still have a team that does not trust you.

That is what leaders feel when culture erodes while structure survives.

---

## The real-world cost: you cannot PowerPoint your way through culture

Gallagher uses SHARP as a case study to show what happens when a wicked problem gets treated like a tame one.

The Army built policies, training requirements, and programs. Those are management tools. Necessary tools.

But his point is that policy alone does not create cultural change. He cites findings that stronger leadership commitment was needed, and that early efforts pushed policy without the leadership emphasis required to shift behavior.

That lesson applies far beyond SHARP.

You cannot slide-deck your way into trust.
You cannot tracker your way into respect.
You cannot spreadsheet your way into a healthy culture.

Those outcomes are built through consistent leadership behavior over time.

---

## Balance is not optional

Effective organizations understand leadership and management are complementary.

Management defines how work happens.
Leadership defines why it happens.

Management stabilizes.
Leadership mobilizes.

Together, they create an environment where:

- Expectations are clear
- Purpose is understood
- Accountability exists without fear
- Autonomy is supported by structure

Gallagher’s deeper warning is that when we fail to separate these, we reach for management tools by default. Over time, leaders lose the time and energy needed to provide purpose, direction, influence, and motivation.

When that happens, the Army does not just get tired.

It gets less human.

---

## A quick self-check for leaders

If you are responsible for people, these questions reveal whether you are leading, managing, or substituting one for the other:

- Am I trying to solve an uncertain people-problem with control mechanisms?
- Have I confused “visibility” with “progress”?
- Do my systems simplify work or bury it?
- Do my Soldiers understand purpose, or only requirements?
- Do my meetings produce clarity, or just updates?

If the answer keeps pointing to more process and less purpose, that is usually the signal.

You are managing.

You might need to lead.

---

## Conclusion

Leadership and management are not opposing forces.

They are distinct responsibilities that must work together.

Management ensures reliability.
Leadership ensures engagement.

Gallagher’s argument is that the Army often muddies the difference. When we do, we end up trying to solve dynamic problems with management processes, and we underutilize the leadership behaviors that actually drive lasting change.

In modern organizations, that distinction is not abstract.

It is the difference between:

- Trust and turnover  
- Culture and compliance  
- Long-term strength and gradual decline  

Knowing when to apply structure, and when to lead with purpose, is not optional.

It is foundational.
